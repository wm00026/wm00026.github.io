# Building Skills in Cybersecurity (and Related Fields)
Cybersecurity is one of the many fields where hands on, practical experience matters as much, or perhaps more, than theoretical frameworks. Of course, the theoretical frameworks help frame our understanding of how we can approach practical applications of skills, but skills are incredibly important. 

Knowing what the TCP and UDP protocols are, how they work, and their pros and cons is good to know. But not knowing how to use the TCP and UDP protocols doesn't help us at all. Just like knowing how to implement a breath first search is useful, but not knowing what a breath first search is, why we use it, and its pros and cons isn't really helpful. Both play an important role in doing work, whatever work that may be.

We'll discuss building cybersecurity skills and knowledge here in this blog post

## Practical Knowledge v. "Textbook Knowledge"

There's a difference between what will be practical and in use everyday and what can be called "textbook knowledge". Things you learn in a textbook that aren't necessarily "practical" in everyday use cases. That doesn't mean the textbook knowledge is bad or useless; it can add context to the more practical knowledge, show a progression of how we got here, or be useful is different use cases. But it may be important to make the distinction. 

We can go back to the TCP example to see exactly how this would play out. I'll be referring to the [TCP RFC](https://www.ietf.org/rfc/rfc9293.html) for what can be determined as "practical knowledge. The RFC details the specification for the TCP format,security and privacy considerations, and implementation notes. We could classify this as practical knowledge; this gives us information on how we would implement or understand how to work with the TCP protocol.

Now, for instance, if we discussed the history of TCP and looked at the [TCP Version 2 specification](https://history.rfc-editor.org/ien/ien5.pdf) under the Internet Experiment Note (IEN). This is an outdated version of TCP and would not be very useful in our modern implementation of TCP. But, the IEN is useful for understanding how we evolved and developed TCP from its first iteration to the current iteration. We would classify this was "textbook knowledge". 

Something can also be textbook knowledge and practical knowledge. For example the [CIA Triad](https://www.fortinet.com/resources/cyberglossary/cia-triad) is often one of the first things a cybersecurity student learns in their first cybersecurity course. It's often reinforced constantly through more topics and subjects. This is textbook knowledge. But, on a practical level, the CIA triad plays a constant role on how we move about in cybersecurity. The practical implementation of different security protocols, like access control (MAC, RBAC, etc.) surround confidentiality, integrity, and availability.

### Avoiding "Larp"

In cybersecurity, it's often the case that we want to use our knowledge to help others. But there tends to be a point of minimal value at a certain point. We can use all the tools and skills and knowledge we have to give a user advice on tools or programs to use; VPNs or browsers or software. But if they don't use it, or don't implement it, or find that they lose access to things they want to do or use, then the value of the security implementation is lost.

It's important to strike a balance between "fort knox" and "password123 for every password". Practical, useful knowledge that is explained well is better than running your OS on a USB drive.

## Practicing Skills By Implementing Practical Projects
For the most part, the best way to practice knowledge and skills is doing something. Running a VM, doing a home lab, doing CTF activities and performing write ups are some of the best ways to practice skills we learn in the classroom. That doesn't mean you have to run the most insane and intense projects. Running your homelab is a perfect way to practice cybersecurity skills. 

Simply implementing programs or software, running VMs, managing users and access can show that you understand the principles of cybersecurity and have the skills (or at least the curiosity to learn the skills) to implement those principles. The best thing to do is to do something you're *interested* in, have a curious mind, and practice the skills you've learned.
